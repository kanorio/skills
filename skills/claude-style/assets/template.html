    Essay / 12 min read

    The quote you almost cut

    The line that does not fit your themes is often the most useful one in the study. Notes on keeping it, and on what it costs to throw it away.

    ICInes Calloway
    November 18, 2026

    Every study has one. You are three weeks in, the themes have settled, the memo is half written, and you reread an interview to find a cleaner version of a quote. Instead you find a line that does not fit anywhere. It is not wrong. It is just inconvenient.

    The easy move is to leave it out. It belongs to one participant, it complicates a clear story, and nobody reading the memo will know it was there. This essay is an argument for keeping it, or at least for deciding on purpose.

    Why the odd line matters

    1We call these “orphan quotes” in the workspace: coded lines that belong to no theme. They get their own list so they do not disappear.

    Themes are built by grouping what people said in similar ways1. That is their strength and their blind spot. A theme rewards repetition, so a single sharp observation can lose to five vague ones.

    A theme rewards repetition. The odd line is often where the study is trying to tell you something new.

    In our clinic study, one porter said the paper list on the ward wall was “the real system, and everyone pretends it isn’t.” Nobody else said it. It became the most discussed finding in the final review.

    Three ways to keep it honestly

    1. Give it its own heading in the memo, labelled as a single voice, so readers can weigh it themselves.
    2. Go back to the other interviews and search for the idea in different words. It is often there, said quietly.
      2Search by meaning helps here: describe the idea, not the words the participant used.
    3. Write down why you left it out, if you do. A one-line note is enough, and it keeps the decision visible to your team.

    None of this slows a study much. What it changes is the habit: the memo stops being the story you found first and becomes the record of what people actually said.

    Customers/Northmere Health Trust

    Sixty clinic interviews, one plan, three weeks

    How a four-person research team rebuilt night handover around what staff actually trust.

    The question

    Northmere's wards had three handover tools and a paper list that none of them replaced. Leadership wanted to know which one to keep. The research team suspected that was the wrong question.

    The work

    The team interviewed sixty nurses, porters, clerks and doctors across four sites, mostly on night shifts. Two researchers coded each interview, compared their codes and settled differences in review.

    Shared codebook / 4 of 31 codes
    Trust in people88 quotes
    Paper as backup41 quotes
    Alert fatigue37 quotes
    Handover by name29 quotes

    What changed

    The finding was not about tools at all: staff trusted notes from people they knew. The new handover keeps a named author on every entry, and the paper list became an official, printed page.

    “For the first time, the board could read what nurses said, in their words, next to every recommendation.”Tomas Brenner, head of patient experience research

    About

    We build tools for people who listen

    Wrenfield started in a university basement, with two researchers, a hundred printed transcripts and a wall of sticky notes that fell down every night.

    What we believe

    1. 01

      Evidence before eloquence

      A finding is only as good as the quotes behind it. We make the trail easy to follow and hard to lose.

    2. 02

      Quiet by default

      No streaks, no badges, no noise. Research needs long stretches of attention, so the software stays out of the way.

    3. 03

      Your data stays yours

      Participants trusted you with their words. We never train on them, sell them or keep them after you leave.
