Questions we hear first

Do you work with older machines?

Yes. Most lines we connect are ten to thirty years old. Where a controller cannot share data, we fit sensors on the outside instead of opening the machine.

Where does our data live?

On a server in your plant or in your own cloud account. We never keep your production data on ours, and you keep every login when we leave.

Which systems do you connect to?

Common controllers, historians, ERP and maintenance systems. We confirm the list for your plant during the site assessment.

How long until we see results?

First live data usually arrives in three weeks. A pilot runs ten to twelve weeks, so the measurement covers normal production and a changeover or two.

Can our own team take over?

That is the goal. Every engagement ends with documentation, training for two shifts and a handover checklist your maintenance lead signs.

The challenge

Stops were logged on paper at the end of each shift, so the reasons blurred into "mechanical" and "other". The weekly meeting argued about numbers instead of fixing machines.

What we did

  • Read the existing controllers on all eleven lines and fitted 64 sensors to the ovens, proofers and wrappers they could not see.
  • Put a reason picker on a tablet at each line, so operators log a stop in two taps while it happens.
  • Built one dashboard per shift and one per site, and tuned alerts with the technicians over four weeks.

The result

Tuesday turned out to be the day the main proofer was cleaned with a rushed restart. A changed procedure and one sensor alert removed most of the stops. Across all sites, unplanned stops fell by 31% in fourteen weeks.

For the first time the weekly meeting starts with the same numbers on every screen.

Guide · Maintenance

A practical guide to vibration monitoring on older lines

DODev Okafor, principal engineer
2026-11-04 · 12 min read

On this page

Why vibration firstChoosing sensorsSetting a baselineAlerts people trust

Most rotating equipment tells you it is about to fail. On older lines nobody is listening, because the machines were built before anyone thought to ask.

Why vibration first

Bearings, gearboxes, fans and pumps cause a large share of unplanned stops on the lines we see, and almost all of them change how they vibrate long before they stop. A small sensor on the outside of the housing hears that change without touching the controller.

Choosing sensors

Start with the ten assets whose failure stops a whole line. For each one, pick the simplest sensor that can hear the fault you fear most.

AssetSensorTypical warning
Motor bearingTriaxial accelerometer7 to 14 days
GearboxAccelerometer and oil temperature5 to 10 days
FanAccelerometer and current clamp10 to 20 days
PumpAccelerometer and pressure3 to 7 days

Setting a baseline

Record two weeks of normal running before you set a single alert. Include a cleaning cycle, a changeover and a cold start, or the first of each will look like a fault.

Alerts people trust

  • Send each alert to one named person, never to a shared inbox.
  • Say what to check and how urgent it is, in the words your technicians use.
  • Review every alert at the weekly meeting for the first two months, and delete the ones nobody acted on.

An alert that fires twice for nothing gets ignored the third time, when it matters.

Dev Okafor
