    Questions.

    Are the open models really free to use commercially?

    Yes. The weights are released under Apache 2.0, so you can run, change and sell services built on them. The plans pay for our hosted API, the field app and support.

    What counts as a field?

    A field is one saved location with its own forecast and alerts. Any size works: a single glasshouse, an orchard block or a solar array.

    What happens if we go over our API calls?

    Calls keep working for the rest of the month and we email you. If it happens twice in a row, we suggest the next plan; we never switch it for you.

    Can we change plans or cancel at any time?

    Yes. Changes apply from the next billing period, and your saved fields and history stay available to export.

    Quickstart

    Get a forecast for one field in about five minutes, from a terminal or from code.

    Install

    Install the Python client. The command-line tool comes with it.

    Terminal
    pip install fieldlark

    Authenticate

    Create a key on the sign-up page and keep it in an environment variable. Every request sends it in the Authorization header.

    Keep your key on the server. Anyone with it can spend your monthly calls.

    Your first forecast

    Ask for the next six hours at one point. Coordinates are decimal degrees.

    curl https://api.fieldlark.example/v1/forecast \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $FIELDLARK_KEY" \
  -d '{"lat": 52.09, "lon": 5.12, "model": "nowcast-2", "hours": 6}'
    from fieldlark import Client

client = Client()  # reads FIELDLARK_KEY
forecast = client.forecast(lat=52.09, lon=5.12, model="nowcast-2", hours=6)
print(forecast.hours[0].rain)

    Read the response

    Each hour carries the most likely value and a band that holds the true value eight times in ten.

    FieldTypeMeaning
    tstringLocal time at the field
    rainnumberChance of rain, 0 to 1
    bandarrayLow and high values of the 80% band

      Why a small model beats a global one in your own field

      NHNoor Halvorsen·12 November 2026·8 min read

      Global weather models are built for a grid cell the size of a town, and an orchard block is not a town. For a year we ran Nowcast-2 next to two global forecasts on 41 farms and compared both with what actually fell.

      What we measured

      We scored the chance of rain for each of the next six hours against station gauges, and the overnight minimum against a sensor at the lowest point of each field.

      • 41 farms in three climates, from one season of daily runs.
      • Rain scored hourly, frost scored nightly.
      • Every model given the same radar and the same stations.

      Where the small model wins

      The gap is widest in the first two hours and in fields with their own shape: a valley floor, a south slope, a treeline that holds the cold in.

      The forecast that matters is the one for the corner where the frost settles, not the average of the whole valley.

      Where it still misses

      Past two days the global models catch up and then pull ahead. That is why our seasonal outlooks start from them and only adjust for your field. Read the quickstart to try both on your own coordinates.
