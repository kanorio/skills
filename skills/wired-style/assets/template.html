Oceans / Managed retreat

The Harbor Town That Voted to Move Back From the Sea

After three floods in four winters, Port Ellery chose to hand its waterfront to the tide. The vote took one evening. The move will take a generation.

By Maren Holt

/

Nov 23, 2026

/

12 min read

The meeting ran past midnight, which nobody in Port Ellery found surprising. What surprised people was the count. Of the 412 residents who filled the school gym on a wet Tuesday in March, 301 raised a hand for a plan that, a decade earlier, would have ended a political career: stop rebuilding the lower harbor, and start moving it uphill.

“We did the math out loud,” says Ada Corrigan, who has run the chandlery on Quay Street for twenty-six years. “Three floods, three repairs, three insurance letters. The fourth letter was the one that said they would not send a fifth.”

A Line on Every Wall

Every building on the lower harbor carries the same stripe of salt and silt, about a meter up the wall, left by the flood of the second winter. Some owners painted over it. Most did not. On the ferry office, someone has written the date beside it in marker, and below that, smaller, a question mark.

The plan the town adopted is called managed retreat, a phrase planners have used for decades and residents tend to dislike. In practice it is a schedule. Over twenty years, homes and businesses move to land the town already owns above the old rail line. The lower streets become a park that is allowed to flood, and the sea wall, instead of being raised again, is lowered in two places so the water has somewhere to go.

“We are not losing the harbor. We are deciding where the water goes before it decides for us.”Chair of the harbor council

Who Pays to Move a Town

Moving is expensive, and the money arrives slowly. The first phase, a new quay and twenty-four homes, is funded. The rest depends on grants that are awarded one year at a time. Planners say the order matters more than the speed: the families with the least room to absorb another flood move first.

Not everyone is going. A handful of owners on the waterfront have said they will stay and raise their houses on new piles. The plan allows it, on one condition: after a storm, the town will no longer rebuild the road in front of them. One of them told me she had made peace with being, in her words, the last house on the map.

What Other Towns Are Watching

Port Ellery is small enough that its choice will not change national policy. It is watched anyway. Planners from three coastal counties have visited since the vote, and their questions are practical:

  • how the town priced the land uphill;
  • what it offered the families who rent rather than own;
  • and what it told the people whose work depends on being next to the water.

The chandlery is scheduled to move in the third year. Corrigan has already picked the spot: a corner on the new quay, close enough to see the boats, and a meter and a half higher than her current floor. “Higher than the line,” she says. “That is the whole plan, really.”

About

About Tidemark

Tidemark is a magazine about climate, oceans and cities, written for people who live near water and for people who just want to know where it is going. We report from the coast, explain the science plainly, and correct ourselves in public.

What We Cover

Sea level, storms and heat; the science behind them; and the towns, farms and cities adapting in real time.

How We Report

On the ground where we can, with named sources wherever possible, and with every number checked against its source.

How We Are Paid For

By readers. Subscriptions fund the newsroom, and no one pays for coverage or sees a story before it runs.

Editorial standards

  1. We correct mistakes in the open

    Corrections are added to the story with the date and what changed. Report an error

  2. We name our sources

    When we cannot, we say why, and at least two editors know who the source is.

  3. We separate news from opinion

    Opinion pieces carry an "Opinion" label above the headline, every time.

  4. We do not take money for coverage

    Advertisers and partners never see or approve a story before it is published.
